## The photo that set you up

The picture that sold you on this trip was almost certainly a long exposure. The camera sat on a tripod, on solid ground, with the shutter open for ten, twenty or thirty seconds, drinking in light the whole time. The water in those shots runs electric blue, bright enough to read by.

Your eyes do not work that way. From a kayak, what you see is a blue-white sparkle that flashes where the water moves and dies back to black a second later. On a good night it is genuinely bright. It is still paler and dimmer than the postcard.

That gap is physics, not a scam. A sensor collecting thirty seconds of faint light will always out-gather a human eye taking the scene moment by moment, and it renders the blue deeper than night vision does. Knowing this before you go changes two things: you stop expecting the postcard, and you start to understand what a camera has to do to get anywhere near it.

## Quick settings cheat sheet

| Situation | Settings to start with |
|---|---|
| Phone, braced on a dock or paddle | Night mode, flash off, Live Photo off, 3 to 10 seconds if your phone lets you choose |
| Camera on a tripod, on land | Manual mode, widest aperture, ISO 1600 to 3200, 10 to 30 seconds, manual focus on infinity |
| Camera in the kayak | About 0.5 to 2 seconds at f/2.8 to f/4, ISO 1600 to 6400, bracket several frames |

Brightness varies a lot with the number of organisms, how much the water is disturbed and how much light is around, so treat these as starting points and adjust.

## Why the glow defeats auto mode

Start with the light itself. The flash is faint blue, around 480 nanometres, made by plankton when the water around them is disturbed. The full mechanism is covered in [What is bioluminescence?](/guides/what-is-bioluminescence/) The short version: it only registers in real darkness. Moonlight washes it out, which is why [moon phase and bioluminescence](/guides/moon-phase-and-bioluminescence/) matters as much as any camera setting, and why the [moon phase calendar](/moon-phase-calendar/) is worth checking before you book a date.

The second problem is that the subject switches itself off. Still water does not glow. A calm lagoon photographs black because nothing in it is lit. Every usable frame has movement in it: a paddle stroke, a bow wake or a hand trailed alongside the hull.

The third problem is the phone in your pocket. In auto mode, it keeps exposures short to protect against hand shake, may fire a flash, and sends autofocus hunting through the dark for something to lock onto. Short exposure, stray flash, missed focus: auto mode does everything wrong at once.

## What a phone can honestly do

Night mode is the whole game. It stretches the exposure across several seconds and combines the frames, which is the only reason a phone records any glow at all. On an iPhone, the night mode icon appears in dark scenes, and you can drag the slider to a longer time. On Android phones, switch to Night or Night Sight. Four habits decide whether it works:

1. **Turn the flash off and leave it off.** A flash lights up the kayak and the water directly in front of you, and kills the faint blue behind it. It also wipes out the night vision of everyone paddling near you. Turn off Live Photo too.
2. **Brace the phone.** Rest it against a paddle shaft, a dry bag or the dock before you launch. The longer the exposure, the more a tiny shake blurs it. A cheap phone tripod helps if you shoot from shore.
3. **Use the main lens.** The standard 1x camera has the best sensor. Skip digital zoom.
4. **Shoot the disturbance, not the bay.** Press the shutter as someone paddles through the frame or trails a hand in the water. A frame of undisturbed water is a frame of black.

Phones with a pro mode can go further. Set a long exposure, ISO around 1600 to 3200, and shoot RAW if you plan to edit afterwards. Taking five to ten frames in a row and merging them in editing software, called stacking, improves quality.

Video is the weak option. It chops the scene into short frames, each one collecting a fraction of the light a long still exposure gathers, so a glow that looks clear to your eyes often barely registers. Take stills first, and shoot a clip afterwards with low expectations.

## Camera settings that work

If you brought a dedicated camera, the recipe is settled: manual mode, a tripod, the lens at its widest aperture and manual focus. Autofocus has nothing to find in the dark, so set focus on a distant light or the horizon before full dark, then leave it alone. Turn off image stabilisation when the camera sits on a tripod. Fire the shutter with a remote release or the self-timer, so your finger does not shake the first second of the exposure. Shoot RAW.

Published settings vary because the glow varies:

- **High ISO, wide aperture.** Marine photographer Doug Perrine used 30-second exposures at ISO 2500 to 6400, with apertures from f/3.5 to f/5.6, on a wide-angle lens. The Jervis Bay guide from Tasman Holiday Parks starts photographers at ISO 1600 to 3200, f/2.8 and 5 to 30 seconds.
- **Low ISO, long exposure.** Other guides start lower, at ISO 200 to 400 with f/4 to f/8 and 30 to 60 seconds, to keep noise down on bright displays.

Begin at the gentle end: widest aperture, ISO 3200, 15 seconds. If the frame is too dark, lengthen the exposure before you raise the ISO, since grain climbs fast at the top of the range. Review the first few shots and adjust. Time the exposure to movement: open the shutter as a paddle enters the water, so the stroke draws its trail across the frame.

## The kayak problem

Every setting above assumes the camera is holding still. A kayak never holds still. Even sitting motionless, you drift, rock and turn with the current. A ten-second exposure from the boat smears the shoreline into fog and spreads the paddle trail until it disappears.

From a kayak you have two honest choices:

- **Keep exposures short.** Try about half a second to two seconds at f/2.8 to f/4 and ISO 1600 to 6400, and take several frames to cover the movement. Expect soft, grainy pictures that read as atmosphere rather than detail.
- **Do the serious shooting from solid ground.** Use the dock and shoreline at the launch, before you push off and after you land, with another paddler moving through the frame for you.

That holds at [Cocoa Beach](/florida/cocoa-beach/) and at [Mosquito Bay](/puerto-rico/mosquito-bay-vieques/) alike. The boat is where you see the glow best. The shore is where you photograph it.

## Protect the gear

Water gets into everything on a night paddle. Keep your phone or camera in a dry bag or waterproof pouch when you are not shooting, and clip or strap it to you so it cannot sink. Some Puerto Rico operators suggest leaving cameras and valuables behind, so check your operator's policy before you pack. Our [what to wear and bring](/guides/what-to-wear-and-bring/) guide covers the dry-bag setup.

## Light discipline

No flash. No white torch. No phone screen at full brightness while you fumble for the camera app. Every burst of white light costs you the dark adaptation you spent twenty minutes building, and it costs the whole group theirs.

If you need to find a zipper or a lens cap, use a red light. Red light leaves night vision largely intact, which is why night paddlers and astronomers carry it. Keep your camera screen dim, and review your shots in one batch rather than after every frame. Your eyes are the main instrument on this trip. The camera is borrowing them.

## Put it away for the last stretch

Full dark adaptation takes about twenty minutes, and one look at a bright screen restarts a good part of that clock. So split the night deliberately:

1. **Early:** photograph from the dock if you want to, while the group launches.
2. **Middle:** paddle with the camera stowed and your phone in a dry bag.
3. **Last stretch:** give it to your eyes only.

This is not sentiment. The sparkle your camera keeps missing is tuned for an eye that has sat in the dark, undisturbed, for twenty minutes. Late in the tour, on a moonless night, that eye will show you more of the bay than any screen on the boat. The photographs are for later. The last twenty minutes are the part you actually came for.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can you photograph bioluminescence with a phone?**
Yes, but with limits. Use night mode, turn the flash off and hold the phone completely still. Expect a dimmer, noisier result than a tripod-mounted camera.

**What camera settings are best for bioluminescence?**
Manual mode, widest aperture, ISO 1600 to 3200, 10 to 30 seconds on a tripod. From a kayak, use about 0.5 to 2 seconds at a higher ISO.

**Why does my photo look brighter than what I saw?**
A long exposure gathers light for seconds, while your eye sees the scene moment by moment.

**Can you photograph bioluminescence from a kayak?**
Yes, with short exposures and soft results. For sharper images, shoot from the dock or shore.

**Should I use flash?**
No. It overpowers the faint glow and ruins everyone's night vision.

## The short version

Set your expectations to the eye, not the postcard. Use night mode, turn off the flash, brace the phone and shoot the disturbance. Take the serious long exposures from shore, and spend the last stretch of the paddle with your eyes only.
