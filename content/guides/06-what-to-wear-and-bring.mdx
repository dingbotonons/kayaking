## You will get wet. Count on it

Nobody has to fall in. The water arrives anyway, one paddle stroke at a time.

Every stroke lifts a wet blade over the kayak, and the drip runs down the shaft and across your legs. Do that for two hours and your lap is soaked. Add spray on a choppy night, a damp seat and a launch from a beach, mud bank or low dock, and you climb out of the boat wet from the waist down. On a windy night, wet to the shoulders.

That does not spoil a tour. It spoils the drive home, and only if you dressed for a dry evening out. Everything on this list follows from one fact: wear clothes that behave well when wet, protect the few things that cannot get wet, and leave a dry version of yourself waiting in the car.

## Quick packing list by destination

| | Florida | Puerto Rico | Washington |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clothes | Summer: shorts and a quick-dry top. Winter: long quick-dry pants, long sleeves, one warm layer | Swimsuit or quick-dry shorts and top, one thin layer | Synthetic or wool base layer, warm mid layer, wind and rain shell |
| Feet | Water shoes or strapped sandals | Water shoes or strapped sandals | Closed-toe shoes that can get wet |
| On your skin | Repellent is your call | Operators usually ask for no sunscreen or chemical repellent | Follow your operator's guidance |
| Car kit | Dry clothes, towel | Dry clothes, towel | Dry clothes, towel, warm layer |

## What to wear, by region and season

The base outfit is the same everywhere: quick-dry shorts, leggings or paddle pants, and a quick-dry top. Synthetic fabrics hold little water and dry on your body. Cotton is the mistake. A cotton T-shirt and jeans soak up water, stay cold and heavy, and are still damp when you reach your hotel.

**Florida in summer** is the easy case. Tours around [Cocoa Beach](/florida/cocoa-beach/) run in hot, humid weather, and shorts with a T-shirt or rash guard are enough.

**Florida in winter** is a different night. The comb jelly season runs October to April, and the air cools after dark. Long quick-dry pants, long sleeves and one light warm layer beat shorts. You sit still in a boat, on water, in the dark, so dress one layer warmer than you would for the same evening on land.

**Puerto Rico** stays warm all year, and the dry season from December to mid-April is the most reliable stretch for the glow. A swimsuit or shorts and a light top cover most nights. Bring one thin layer for the ride back if you run cold.

**Washington** is the cold case, and it is not close. Tours around [Orcas Island](/washington/orcas-island/) and San Juan Island run June to September. The water stays cold all year, and operators advise dressing for the water temperature rather than the air temperature, since a splash cools you fast. Long pants, a warm mid layer and a windproof shell are the baseline, not an upgrade.

One nuance: many Washington tours use sea kayaks with spray skirts, which keep most water out of the boat, so you may stay drier below the waist than in a Florida sit-on-top. Your feet still get wet at launch, and you still need warm layers on the water.

## Feet, and the bag that saves your phone

Wear shoes that stay on your feet and are built to get wet: water shoes, sport sandals with a heel strap, or old trainers you do not mind soaking. Flip-flops slide off in mud and on wet docks, and several Washington operators say to leave them at home. Bare feet are a gamble at launch points, which can be gravel, shell or a slick ramp rather than soft sand.

Your phone, keys and wallet need one waterproof home between them. A small dry bag is the standard answer. A waterproof phone pouch on a lanyard works if you want the phone reachable, and keeps it off the floor of the boat, where the puddle collects. Some operators supply dry bags and ask you to fit everything inside them, so pack light.

A simple test cuts most packing lists down to size: everything you carry should be something you are willing to fish out of a wet hull in the dark.

## Lights: red is the polite setting

Your eyes take about twenty minutes to adapt fully to the dark, and one blast of white light resets that for you and everyone near you. The glow you came to see is faint blue light in black water, and white light on top of it washes it out.

Most of the night, you need no light of your own. Guides carry lights and use them at the launch, at the briefing and on the way back in. If you own a headlamp, set it to red and point it down on the walk from the car and around the dock.

Check your operator's rules, though. Some tours require every paddler to bring a headlamp or flashlight but ask you to use it as little as possible. The worst offender is the phone torch: bright, white, and usually switched on at face height. Leave the phone in the dry bag and let your eyes do the work. What guides carry for the group is covered in [Is bioluminescent kayaking safe?](/guides/is-bioluminescent-kayaking-safe/)

## Puerto Rico: nothing on your skin

The bio bay tours in Puerto Rico add one rule that surprises people: do not put anything on your skin before you get on the water.

Operators at [Mosquito Bay](/puerto-rico/mosquito-bay-vieques/) and Laguna Grande commonly ask guests to skip sunscreen, lotion and chemical insect repellent. Alikei Adventures, which runs clear kayak tours on Mosquito Bay, tells guests not to use any sunscreen or insect repellent before the tour, because harsh chemicals pollute the bay. Pure Adventure at Laguna Grande asks guests to avoid lotions and sunscreen for the same reason. It is an operator condition stated when you book, so treat it as part of the tour.

The reasoning is short. Whatever is on your skin washes off in the water. These are small, enclosed bays, and their value is the concentration of dinoflagellates living in them. What washes in stays in. After dark, sunscreen has no job to do anyway.

The honest tension is mosquitoes. Mosquito Bay lives up to its name, and shorelines at mangrove launches can be busy at dusk. Guidance varies by operator and bay. Some provide eco-friendly repellent on site, and others recommend creams or balms over sprays. Ask when you book and follow what your guide says. Cover up with long sleeves and long pants at the launch, and apply repellent after the tour for the walk back to the car.

Swimming is a separate rule. In Puerto Rico, La Parguera is the one bay where swimming is allowed. Swimming is not allowed in Mosquito Bay or Laguna Grande.

In Florida, where operators do not set this rule, repellent before the tour is your call.

## What stays in the car

If you cannot afford to watch it sink in dark water, it does not get in the kayak. That means expensive watches, loose jewellery, a wallet fat with cards and a second phone. Prescription glasses go on a retainer strap. Car keys go in the dry bag, clipped or zipped, never loose in a pocket that will be underwater if you lean wrong.

Then pack the car, not the boat. A complete dry change of clothes waits for you at the end of the night, underwear included, because the wet gets through everything. Add a towel and, in Florida's winter season or anywhere in Washington, a warm layer for the drive. Getting back into dry clothes in a parking lot at 11 pm decides whether the night ends well.

With children, double the spare clothes. A wet adult is uncomfortable. A wet child in an air-conditioned car is cold within minutes. The full family version of this list is in [Bioluminescent kayaking with kids](/guides/bioluminescent-kayaking-with-kids/).

## Frequently asked questions

**What should I wear for a bioluminescent kayak tour?**
Quick-dry synthetic clothes and water shoes. Add layers in cooler regions and seasons. Avoid cotton.

**Will I get wet on a bioluminescent kayak tour?**
Yes, almost certainly. Paddle drip alone will soak your lap, and launches are often from a beach or low dock.

**Can I wear sunscreen or bug spray?**
In Puerto Rico's bio bays, operators generally ask you not to. In other places, follow your operator's guidance.

**Should I bring a flashlight?**
Check your operator. Some require a headlamp, but all want it kept off during the glow. Use red light if you have it.

**What should I leave behind?**
Valuables, loose jewellery and anything you cannot afford to lose in the water. Leave a dry set of clothes and a towel in the car.

## The short version

Wear quick-dry clothes and water shoes, and add layers for cool nights and for Washington. Protect your phone and keys in a dry bag, keep lights low and red, and check your operator's rule about sunscreen and repellent. Then leave a dry change waiting in the car.
