Bioluminescence is light made by a living thing through a chemical reaction. In coastal bays, the glow behind a paddle comes mostly from microscopic plankton that flash when the water around them is disturbed. This guide explains the chemistry, the creatures, why some bays glow far brighter than the open sea, and when to go.

## What is bioluminescence?

Bioluminescence is [light made by a living organism](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/ocean-fact/bioluminescence/) through a chemical reaction. It is rare on land but common in the sea, where [most types of animals, from bacteria to sharks](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/biolum.html), include at least some glowing members.

On land the famous example is the firefly, and [beetle larvae and even some mushrooms](https://www.nps.gov/pore/learn/nature/otherlifeforms.htm) glow too. In the ocean the effect is on a different scale. In the open water column, [about 80 percent of animals living between 200 and 1,000 meters deep](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/ocean-fact/bioluminescence/) are bioluminescent, most commonly fish, squid and gelatinous animals such as jellyfish and comb jellies.

What nobody has fully explained is the bigger picture. [NOAA notes that scientists still do not know](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/ocean-fact/bioluminescence/) why the trait is common in ocean water but missing from freshwater and land, or exactly how it first evolved. The animals are hard to study, because bright lights drive mobile creatures away and many forms of glow cannot be seen under ordinary light. For a paddler, the takeaway is simple: the sparkle on the surface of a bay is a small, reachable piece of a much larger deep-sea story.

## How does bioluminescence work?

Bioluminescence works when an enzyme acts on a light-producing molecule, and the reaction releases energy as light. In the best-known system the molecule is luciferin and the enzyme is [luciferase](https://blogs.ifas.ufl.edu/volusiaco/2023/07/21/creatures-from-the-glowing-lagoon/).

The details differ from species to species. [All bioluminescent organisms use an enzyme and a substrate](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/ocean-fact/bioluminescence/), but different groups use different chemicals, which is why researchers think the ability evolved independently many times. So luciferin is better thought of as a family of light-making molecules than as one single substance.

The reaction also gives off [very little heat](https://www.nps.gov/pore/learn/nature/otherlifeforms.htm), which is why the glow is often called cold light. That is a useful contrast with a light bulb, which wastes much of its energy as warmth. We have kept the claim modest here: sources describe the reaction as producing little heat, but they do not support a precise efficiency figure, so we do not quote one.

## What makes the water glow when you paddle?

The glow behind a paddle comes from dinoflagellates, single-celled plankton that flash light when the water around them is disturbed. [Each flash lasts about a tenth of a second](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/brightest-bioluminescent-bay), then the cell resets.

Dip a paddle on a dark night and the water answers. Each stroke leaves a trail of blue sparks, and a fish darting past draws a bright line. It looks as though the water itself is lit from inside, but it is not. The light comes from living cells far too small to see one by one. That is why the glow follows your paddle and fades the moment you stop: still water on a calm night looks dark until something disturbs it. The description here is the documented mechanism, not a personal anecdote.

Which plankton does the work depends on where you are. In Florida's Indian River Lagoon the organism is [Pyrodinium bahamense](https://blogs.ifas.ufl.edu/volusiaco/2023/07/21/creatures-from-the-glowing-lagoon/), a dinoflagellate that photosynthesizes and swims with a whip-like tail called a flagellum. Its name, [whirling fire](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/brightest-bioluminescent-bay), suits it. Puerto Rico's three famous bays [rely on the same species](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-3397/22/7/311). On California's coast the story changes: at Point Reyes, [the glow in Tomales Bay comes from Noctiluca scintillans](https://www.nps.gov/pore/learn/nature/otherlifeforms.htm), commonly called sea sparkle.

The plankton cannot tell a predator from a kayak. [Paddles, boat engines and even a hand trailed through the water](https://blogs.ifas.ufl.edu/volusiaco/2023/07/21/creatures-from-the-glowing-lagoon/) all trigger the same reaction.

## Why do plankton and animals glow?

Scientists believe bioluminescence mainly helps organisms avoid being eaten, find food and communicate. [NOAA lists the typical uses](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/biolum.html) as warning or evading predators, luring or detecting prey, and signalling to others of the same species, though the purpose is not known for every animal.

For plankton, the leading idea is the burglar alarm. [Researcher Edith Widder uses that phrase](https://www.nps.gov/pore/learn/nature/otherlifeforms.htm): when a grazer nibbles the plankton, the flash lights up the attacker and may attract a bigger predator that hunts the grazer. [UF/IFAS describes the same logic](https://blogs.ifas.ufl.edu/volusiaco/2023/07/21/creatures-from-the-glowing-lagoon/), while noting that scientists are still not certain of the reason.

The picture has a twist for toxic species. A [2024 study in Marine Drugs](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-3397/22/7/311) outlines a hypothesis that, for toxic dinoflagellates at low cell numbers, the flash may act as a warning that the cell is unpalatable, and at high numbers it works more like a burglar alarm. The authors present this as a proposal, not a settled fact.

| Function | What it means | Where it shows up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Avoid being eaten | Startle an attacker, warn it off or call in a bigger predator | Dinoflagellate flashes when disturbed |
| Find or lure food | Detect or attract prey where light is scarce | Animals of the dark water column |
| Communicate | Signal to others of the same species, including for mating | Fireflies on land, many marine animals |

<TableNote>The main documented uses of bioluminescence, summarized from [NOAA](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/biolum.html) and the [National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/pore/learn/nature/otherlifeforms.htm).</TableNote>

## Why do bays glow brighter than the open ocean?

Bays glow brighter because their shape keeps plankton in. [Mosquito Bay's narrow mouth prevents dinoflagellates from being washed out to sea](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/brightest-bioluminescent-bay), and decaying mangroves provide food. In open water, currents spread the same cells thinly across a huge area.

The numbers are striking, but the units matter. Guinness World Records lists Mosquito Bay with [up to 700,000 dinoflagellates per gallon](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/brightest-bioluminescent-bay), which is about 4.5 liters. That works out to roughly 155,000 cells per liter, not 700,000 per liter, a mix-up that is easy to make and that appears on many pages. A separate [University of Puerto Rico thesis](https://scholar.uprm.edu/entities/publication/8dd87c01-0e75-43a5-ba25-3e2619285fc1) that surveyed monthly through 2003 found an annual average of about 286,411 cells per liter in Mosquito Bay and about 50,618 cells per liter in La Parguera's Bahia Bioluminiscente. The two figures come from different methods and years, so treat them as different measurements, not as one contradictory number.

The same thesis adds a useful caution. It found [no significant difference in nutrient levels between the two bays](https://scholar.uprm.edu/entities/publication/8dd87c01-0e75-43a5-ba25-3e2619285fc1), even though plankton numbers and species mix differed, so nutrients alone do not explain which bay is brightest.

Here is how the main U.S. glow spots compare. Brightness is not ranked with numbers because we have no sourced figure for it.

Plan around the core summer months and treat the edges as a bonus.

## How common is bioluminescence in the ocean?

Bioluminescence is extremely common in the ocean's open water. [Around 80 percent of animals between 200 and 1,000 meters deep](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/ocean-fact/bioluminescence/) make their own light, and it is usually blue because blue light travels best through water.

Among surface plankton the trait is rarer than you might expect. In a [2024 research paper](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-3397/22/7/311), the authors note that of roughly 1,555 marine dinoflagellate species, only about 70 are bioluminescent, and about 100 produce toxins. That makes the species that light up our bays a small and special group.

## Why is the glow blue-green, and why do photos look brighter?

The glow looks blue because [blue light travels best through water](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/ocean-fact/bioluminescence/), though it can range from nearly violet to green-yellow and very occasionally red. Photos look brighter than real life because a camera collects light for many seconds, while your eyes do not.

The photograph that sells the trip was almost certainly a long exposure: a camera on a tripod, shutter open for ten, twenty or thirty seconds, drinking in light the whole time. Your eyes do not work that way. From a kayak you see a blue-white sparkle that flashes where the water moves and dies back to black a second later. On a good night it is genuinely bright, but it is still paler and dimmer than the postcard. That gap is physics, not a scam: a sensor gathering thirty seconds of faint light will always out-collect a human eye taking the scene moment by moment.

Published field settings make the point concrete. Marine photographer Doug Perrine worked at 30-second exposures with ISO 2500 to 6400 for bioluminescent plankton, and our photography guide documents similar settings, including a roughly 20-minute dark adaptation for your own eyes. We do not have a side-by-side photo of our own, so the mechanism is the explanation. See the [camera settings in our guide to photographing bioluminescence](/guides/how-to-photograph-bioluminescence).

## Is bioluminescent water safe to kayak in?

Yes for paddlers. [UF/IFAS says Pyrodinium bahamense does not cause direct impacts to people](https://blogs.ifas.ufl.edu/volusiaco/2023/07/21/creatures-from-the-glowing-lagoon/), and you do not need to worry about health effects from being on a boat or kayak in a bloom. The toxin concern is about eating certain seafood, not about paddling.

The seafood concern is real in Florida. [Saxitoxin was identified in Florida populations of this species over the past two decades](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-3397/22/7/311), and UF/IFAS explains that it can build up in shellfish and puffer fish, so [puffer fish harvesting is prohibited in the Indian River Lagoon](https://blogs.ifas.ufl.edu/volusiaco/2023/07/21/creatures-from-the-glowing-lagoon/) and shellfish areas are monitored. In Puerto Rico, the same research notes the bay populations [are not routinely tested for toxicity and are generally assumed non-toxic](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-3397/22/7/311), although cells there carry the genes for making the toxin. That is a reason to be a careful reader, not a reason to avoid a tour.

The bigger safety question for visitors is swimming. Most people assume they can swim wherever the water glows, but at Mosquito Bay they cannot. The bay and its mangroves form the Vieques Bioluminescent Bay Natural Reserve, managed by Puerto Rico's DRNA. Entering the water is prohibited and tours run only with DRNA-licensed operators. The stated reason is what washes off a swimmer, such as sunscreen, repellent and lotions, in a shallow, enclosed bay whose glow rests on an unusually dense plankton population. La Parguera is the exception, where swimming is generally allowed. Read full details in our guide on [swimming in bioluminescent water and DRNA regulations](/guides/can-you-swim-in-bioluminescent-water).

On the water, operator practice matters more than anything else. According to our tour research, Fin Expeditions in Cocoa Beach makes fitted life jackets mandatory on every tour and calls a 30-minute weather hold, or cancels outright, when lightning or high wind threatens, contacting guests about 45 minutes before launch. BK Adventure reschedules every guest in the same conditions. If you paddle on your own, [UF/IFAS advises](https://blogs.ifas.ufl.edu/volusiaco/2023/07/21/creatures-from-the-glowing-lagoon/) wearing a life jacket, carrying proper lighting, filing a float plan, and scouting the route in daylight first.

There is also an ecological footnote. The same UF/IFAS author notes that strong glow seasons can reflect [nitrogen pollution from septic systems, fertilizer and runoff](https://blogs.ifas.ufl.edu/volusiaco/2023/07/21/creatures-from-the-glowing-lagoon/), and that large blooms may signal a decline in the lagoon's health. Research also reports that [Pyrodinium blooms have been lasting longer and growing more intense](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-3397/22/7/311) in Florida. A spectacular night can be both wonderful and a warning.

## When is the best time to see bioluminescence?

The best time is a dark night near the new moon in the warm season. [UF/IFAS recommends going close to the new moon](https://blogs.ifas.ufl.edu/volusiaco/2023/07/21/creatures-from-the-glowing-lagoon/) because moonlight and city light wash out the glow, and the National Park Service says [summer and fall dark cloudy nights](https://www.nps.gov/pore/learn/nature/otherlifeforms.htm) before the moon rises or after it sets are best.

| Condition | Best case | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Moon | A few days either side of new moon | [Too much light, from the moon or cities, hides the glow](https://blogs.ifas.ufl.edu/volusiaco/2023/07/21/creatures-from-the-glowing-lagoon/) |
| Season | Summer and fall at Point Reyes; July to September in Florida's lagoon | [Pyrodinium blooms only in warm water](https://blogs.ifas.ufl.edu/volusiaco/2023/07/21/creatures-from-the-glowing-lagoon/) |
| Weather | No thunderstorms, light winds, or a dark cloudy sky | Safety first, and [clouds darken the sky](https://www.nps.gov/pore/learn/nature/otherlifeforms.htm) |
| Your lighting | Red LED or red-filtered torch only | [White light ruins your night vision](https://www.nps.gov/pore/learn/nature/otherlifeforms.htm) |

<TableNote>Viewing conditions that matter most, sourced from National Park Service and UF/IFAS night-sky guidance.</TableNote>

Here is how that looks on our own moon calendar, which is a published model and not a live feed. New moons fall on 9 November 2026, 9 December 2026 and 7 January 2027. The best windows run 7 to 12 November, 7 to 12 December and 5 to 10 January. Full moons to steer around fall on 24 November and 24 December 2026. If you are reading this on 22 November, the November window has just passed and the full moon is two days away, so the next strong stretch is 7 to 12 December.

The trap is the first-quarter moon, about a week after new moon. It looks half-lit and harmless, but it is up for the whole tour window and sets around midnight. The wrong-weekend cost is real: a group paddling Mosquito Bay under a full moon in the wettest month gets a faint version of the same bay that glows hard three weeks later. See our [moon phase calendar for dark-moon windows](/moon-phase-calendar) and check [tonight's glow forecast](/glow-forecast).

The glow is also uneven from night to night. Research shows [plankton counts fluctuate daily](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-3397/22/7/311) because of bloom growth and decline and water movement, so even a perfect moon does not guarantee a perfect show.

## Frequently asked questions

<FaqAccordion>
  <FaqItem question="Does bioluminescence glow all year?">
    It depends on the place. In our baselines, Mosquito Bay and Laguna Grande glow all year, while Florida's Indian River Lagoon has a dinoflagellate season from about May to September, followed by a softer comb jelly glow from October to April.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="Can you see it without a kayak?">
    Yes, because any disturbance works. [Boat engines, paddles and hands](https://blogs.ifas.ufl.edu/volusiaco/2023/07/21/creatures-from-the-glowing-lagoon/) all trigger it, and at Point Reyes the glow can be [seen anywhere along the National Seashore's coastline](https://www.nps.gov/pore/learn/nature/otherlifeforms.htm) when conditions are right.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="Is it the same as red tide?">
    No. UF/IFAS separates the Indian River Lagoon's glow from the [red tide on Florida's Gulf coast](https://blogs.ifas.ufl.edu/volusiaco/2023/07/21/creatures-from-the-glowing-lagoon/), which can cause fish kills and human health effects. Different organisms are involved, even though both are algal blooms.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="Is bioluminescence hot or harmful to touch?">
    The reaction produces [very little heat](https://www.nps.gov/pore/learn/nature/otherlifeforms.htm), so the water does not feel warmer. At Mosquito Bay, though, you cannot enter the water at all under reserve rules, so touching is for tours that allow it.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="What color is it?">
    Usually blue, since [blue light travels best in water](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/ocean-fact/bioluminescence/). It can range from nearly violet to green-yellow and, very rarely, red.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="Why can't I see it every night?">
    Moonlight, clouds and weather all change what you can see, and [bloom size shifts from day to day](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-3397/22/7/311). The plankton also only flash when the water moves, so a still, calm patch can look completely dark.
  </FaqItem>
</FaqAccordion>
