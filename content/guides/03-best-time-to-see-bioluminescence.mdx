The best time to see bioluminescence is a dark night near the new moon, during your region's warm season, with calm and mostly clear weather. Choose them in that order: season first, moon second, weather last. Get the order wrong and the later filters cannot rescue the earlier ones.

A group paddling Mosquito Bay under a full moon in the wettest month gets a faint version of a bay that glows hard three weeks later. The difference is rarely luck. It comes down to which dates you chose and in what order.

## Filter one: the season

The glow comes from living plankton, and each species keeps its own calendar. If the organism is not in the water in large numbers, nothing else matters. A new moon over an empty bay is just a dark paddle.

Warmth is the common thread. Dinoflagellates are photosynthetic plankton that [bloom in much higher numbers as the water warms](https://www.spokesman.com/stories/2024/dec/12/washington-spot-named-among-top-places-in-world-to), which is why summer dominates most U.S. calendars. In Florida, [UF/IFAS says the lagoon's bright glow is most likely from July to September](https://blogs.ifas.ufl.edu/volusiaco/2023/07/21/creatures-from-the-glowing-lagoon/). Puerto Rico is the exception to the summer rule, because there rain, not temperature, is the constraint.

Season is also the filter with the longest lead time. Flights, ferries and time off get booked months out, so this decision usually comes first anyway. If you want the science of what is actually glowing in each season, [What is bioluminescence?](/guides/what-is-bioluminescence) covers the organisms behind each calendar.

## Filter two: the moon

Once the season is set, narrow the trip to the dark nights around the new moon. Moonlight does not stop the plankton flashing. It competes with a faint blue glow and wins. One Tomales Bay operator [plans its tours around the darkest nights of the season](https://www.virgingifts.com/california/san-francisco/bioluminescent-kayak-adventure-in-tomales-bay) for exactly that reason.

The phase name alone does not tell the whole story. What matters is whether the moon is above the horizon during your tour. These rise and set patterns come from [NASA's explanation of the moon's phases](https://science.nasa.gov/Moon/Moon-phases/) and its [2026 phase visualization](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/5587).

| Moon phase | Rises | Sets | What it means for a night tour |
|---|---|---|---|
| New moon | With the sun | With the sun | Dark all night. The best case. |
| Waxing crescent | Morning | Evening, after sunset | A thin sliver that sets early. Very good after it sets. |
| First quarter | Around midday | Around midnight | Bright through the evening. Dark only after midnight. |
| Full moon | Around sunset | Around sunrise | Up all night. The worst case. |
| Third quarter | Around midnight | Around midday | Dark through the evening, brighter after it rises. |
| Waning crescent | Before dawn | Daytime | Dark until the early hours. Very good. |

<TableNote>Rise and set times from NASA; the interpretation column reflects dark-sky viewing optimization for paddlers.</TableNote>

The trap is the first-quarter moon, about a week after the new moon. It looks half-lit and harmless, but it is high in the sky for the whole early-evening tour window. A lesser-known upside is the third-quarter moon, which does not rise until around midnight, so an early-evening paddle can still be dark. For the deeper mechanics, see [Moon phase & bioluminescence](/guides/moon-phase-and-bioluminescence).

## Filter three: the weather, checked last

Weather is the filter you check in the final days, not the one you plan a season around. Clear, calm nights give the best contrast, and a [Tomales Bay guide describes the glow dimming when nights are too bright or too windy](https://www.onlyinyourstate.com/nature/california/surprise-discovery-bay-norcal). Cloud cover is a mixed blessing: it dims moonlight, and the National Park Service even lists [dark cloudy nights](https://www.nps.gov/pore/learn/nature/otherlifeforms.htm) as good conditions at Point Reyes. Wind is the real problem, because it roughens the water and makes paddling harder.

Rain matters most in Florida and Puerto Rico. In Puerto Rico, [summer thunderstorms can drop heavy rain into the bays](https://matadornetwork.com/read/bioluminescent-kayaking-and-viewing-in-the-us), which is why the driest months are recommended. In our forecast model, more than 25mm of rain in the prior 48 hours caps the Florida and Puerto Rico scores at 60.

Weather is also a safety issue, not just a brightness issue. In 2015, [Marin County firefighters helped rescue 54 kayakers on a bioluminescence paddle](https://www.marincounty.org/main/county-press-releases/press-releases/2015/mcfd-rescue-111515) when conditions on Tomales Bay turned rough, and two people were treated for mild hypothermia. Check the marine forecast in the final days, and if your booking lets you shift a night, this is when you use that option.

## The seasons, region by region

### Florida: Cocoa Beach and Titusville

Dinoflagellates from May to September, peaking in July and August, give the strongest blue sparkle in the Indian River Lagoon. Comb jellies run from October to April with a [softer glow](https://www.onlyinyourstate.com/nature/usa/best-places-to-see-bioluminescence-in-the-us), so the lagoon never goes fully dark in winter. Summer is the pick for brightness, and winter is a calmer, quieter show. See the [Cocoa Beach](/florida/cocoa-beach) page for tonight's score and the tours running there.

### Mosquito Bay, Vieques

The bay glows all year on *Pyrodinium bahamense*. The dry season, December to mid-April, is the most reliable stretch because heavy rain is least likely to dull it. The [Mosquito Bay](/puerto-rico/mosquito-bay-vieques) page carries its season notes and tours.

### Laguna Grande and La Parguera

Laguna Grande in Fajardo also glows all year and is easier to reach than Vieques, since there is no ferry involved. La Parguera, on Puerto Rico's southwest coast, glows all year too, but its tours run by boat rather than kayak.

### Tomales Bay, California

Sources disagree on the edges of the Tomales Bay season, so plan around the middle. One guide has the algae [usually appearing from August through September](https://www.onlyinyourstate.com/nature/california/surprise-discovery-bay-norcal). The National Park Service points to [summer and fall](https://www.nps.gov/pore/learn/nature/otherlifeforms.htm). A guide interviewed by the National Parks Conservation Association said the viewing season [usually ends in early October but ran into December in an unusually dry year](https://www.npca.org/articles/1774-nature-s-night-lights).

There is also a local trick. A Tomales Bay guide says foggy August nights are ideal because the fog [cuts down ambient light from houses](https://online.flippingbook.com/view/107481897/31). Another operator suggests [September or October](https://familyvacationist.com/tomales-bay-california-bioluminescence/) for less wind, a lower chance of rain and earlier sunsets. Details are on the [Tomales Bay](/california/tomales-bay) page.

### San Juan Islands, Washington

The peak is June to September, with July and August the strongest. A San Juan Island operator says the glow [begins in May and intensifies through summer and early fall](https://discoveryseakayak.com/2025/07/san-juan-island-bioluminescent-kayak-tours/). One catch is that in July [lingering daylight pushes tours late](https://discoveryseakayak.com/2025/07/san-juan-island-bioluminescent-kayak-tours/). A Bellingham operator says the plankton live in local water all year, but the summer blooms are what reach the surface in high numbers. She also recommends [calm, protected bays far from city lights](https://www.spokesman.com/stories/2025/jul/31/glowing-plankton-light-up-wa-waves-heres-when-wher) near the new moon. See the [San Juan Island](/washington/san-juan-island) page for the tours that run there.

### San Diego: no calendar, only blooms

San Diego has no dependable season to plan around. The glow comes from red-tide blooms of *Lingulodinium polyedra*, a [dinoflagellate behind the region's red tides, according to Scripps research](https://today.ucsd.edu/story/historic-red-tide-event-of-2020-fueled-by-plankton-super-swimmers). Scripps oceanographer Drew Lucas says the glow can happen [at almost any time of year, with a slight lean toward spring through early fall](https://www.nbcsandiego.com/news/local/when-and-where-to-catch-bioluminescence-in-san-diego/3297836/?amp=1).

Blooms are short and patchy. Lucas says they [typically last about a couple of weeks](https://www.nbcsandiego.com/news/local/when-and-where-to-catch-bioluminescence-in-san-diego/3297836/?amp=1), though the 2020 event lasted almost two months and some are only a night or two. Not every red tide glows, either: [red tides are unpredictable and not all of them produce bioluminescence](https://www.accuweather.com/en/weather-news/mesmerizing-photos-capture-san-diegos-view-of-bioluminescence-turning-the-ocean-aglow/70004916). If you live nearby, react when a bloom is reported. If you are planning from out of town, do not build a trip around San Diego's glow.

## A simple planning timeline

1. **Months ahead:** choose the region and the season, and book flights or ferries.
2. **When you book:** land your tour night inside a new-moon dark window, and check the moon's rise and set times for that date.
3. **The final week:** check the forecast for your bay, and shift a night if the weather turns and your booking allows it.
4. **On the night:** arrive early, give your eyes time to adjust, and use [red light only](https://www.nps.gov/pore/learn/nature/otherlifeforms.htm) to protect your night vision.

## Frequently asked questions

<FaqAccordion>
  <FaqItem question="What is the best month to see bioluminescence?">
    It depends on the region. Florida peaks in July and August, Puerto Rico is most reliable from December to mid-April, Tomales Bay runs roughly August to October, and the San Juan Islands peak from June to September.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="Do I need a new moon to see bioluminescence?">
    No, but it gives the best odds. Moonlight competes with the faint blue glow, so the few nights either side of the new moon are the most reliable. The moon only hurts while it is above the horizon during your tour.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="Can you see bioluminescence in winter?">
    Yes in some places. Puerto Rico glows all year, and Florida's comb jellies give a softer winter glow. Tomales Bay's season [usually ends by early October](https://www.npca.org/articles/1774-nature-s-night-lights), and San Diego is a matter of luck with red tides.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="Does rain affect bioluminescence?">
    Heavy rain can dull the glow in Florida and Puerto Rico, which is why our forecast caps scores after more than 25mm in 48 hours, and [Puerto Rico's summer thunderstorms](https://matadornetwork.com/read/bioluminescent-kayaking-and-viewing-in-the-us) are the reason the dry season is recommended there.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="What time of night is best?">
    Fully dark. A San Diego bioluminescence guide recommends [waiting at least two hours after sunset](https://www.accuweather.com/en/weather-news/mesmerizing-photos-capture-san-diegos-view-of-bioluminescence-turning-the-ocean-aglow/70004916). In Washington in July, tours leave late because of the long daylight.
  </FaqItem>
  <FaqItem question="Can you predict bioluminescence?">
    Only roughly. Plankton numbers change from day to day, and no live feed exists. Use the season and moon to pick the night, then the forecast and the weather to confirm it.
  </FaqItem>
</FaqAccordion>
