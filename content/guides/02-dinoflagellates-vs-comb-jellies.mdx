## Florida's two glows: summer sparkle, winter jellies

Dip a paddle into the Indian River Lagoon in July and the water lights up around the blade. A fish turns and leaves a blue streak behind it. Come back in January and the lagoon is mostly dark, until a clear, walnut-sized shape drifts past, flashing blue-green along eight thin lines.

Those are two different animals. Dinoflagellates make the summer glow. Comb jellies make the winter glow. Both are bioluminescence, and both are worth a night on the water, but they look different and peak in different months. Book for the wrong one and you will still see light, just not the light in the photos.

This split is a Florida story. Puerto Rico's three bays glow all year from a single organism, the dinoflagellate *Pyrodinium bahamense*, with no comb jelly season. The San Juan Islands and Tomales Bay are dinoflagellate shows too.

## The summer show: dinoflagellates

In the Indian River Lagoon, the water behind [Cocoa Beach](/florida/cocoa-beach/) and [Titusville](/florida/titusville/), the glow comes from *Pyrodinium bahamense*. UF/IFAS Extension describes it as the organism behind the lagoon's summer bioluminescence. It is the same species that lights Puerto Rico's bays. It likes warm, salty water, so it builds up through the hot months and fades when the lagoon cools.

When a cell is disturbed, it flashes. The chemistry is covered in [the first guide in this cluster](/guides/what-is-bioluminescence/): a light-making molecule and an enzyme firing a blue flash about a tenth of a second long. Researchers think the flash works as an alarm that startles or exposes predators.

From a kayak, the effect is simple. The cells are microscopic and spread through the whole lagoon, so the glow is everywhere at once. It is not one bright thing. It is the water answering wherever it is moved. If every stroke, ripple and fish trail lights up, you are watching dinoflagellates.

On this site's season baseline, Florida's dinoflagellate season runs May to September and peaks in July and August. Local sources vary a little. UF/IFAS puts the bloom at July to September, and a 2021 monitoring study found blooms generally develop in early summer and fade in autumn. A 2024 paper notes that blooms have been starting earlier and lasting longer in recent years. Treat the dates as a guide, not a guarantee.

## The winter show: comb jellies

Comb jellies are not jellyfish. Jellyfish belong to the phylum Cnidaria. Comb jellies are Ctenophora, a phylum of their own, with no stinging cells. The one behind Florida's winter glow is the sea walnut, *Mnemiopsis leidyi*, which ranges along the Atlantic coast from Maine to Florida.

You can spot one by its eight rows of comb plates running down the body. Each row is made of thousands of tiny beating hairs, called cilia, and the animal swims by rippling them in waves.

Under each row sit light-making cells. When a paddle, a hull or a passing fish jostles the animal, they flash a cold blue-green light that traces the same eight lines. A flash lasts a second or two. It can repeat, but an animal poked again and again dims as it uses up its light-making chemicals.

The chemistry differs from the dinoflagellate flash. Comb jellies use photoproteins, which hold the light-making molecule and the enzyme together in one package and fire when calcium is present. The molecule involved is coelenterazine, the same type used by many jellyfish. The result is a different sight: separate glowing orbs, each the outline of one animal, instead of the whole water lighting up. That is the test for a winter night.

## The rainbow is not the glow

Photos of comb jellies usually show bands of rainbow colour sliding along the comb rows. People often call that bioluminescence. It is not.

The beating cilia split light into colours, much like a compact disc does. NIH researchers who sequenced the sea walnut genome make the same point: the rainbow comes from light reflecting off the cilia, while true bioluminescence is something you only see in the dark.

So here is a quick check. If you see rainbow colours under daylight or a torch, that is scattered light. The real glow is blue-green, and it only shows after dark.

## When each one takes over

The site's baseline puts the changeover in autumn. Dinoflagellates run May to September. Comb jellies take over from October to April, as the water cools and dinoflagellate numbers fall. Some operators stretch the comb jelly window into May. In the transition weeks of late spring and early autumn the lagoon can hold both at once.

One caution on expectations. A Treasure Coast naturalist quoted by the Treasure Coastal Almanac notes that comb jellies also bloom in the lagoon, but in smaller numbers and with less spectacular displays than the summer dinoflagellates. The winter show is real. It is just gentler.

That is why [the glow forecast on this site](/glow-forecast/) never drops to zero in Florida in winter. The lagoon baseline stays moderate through the cold months instead of falling away.

## How to tell which one you will get

**1. Start with the month.** May to September points to dinoflagellates. October to April points to comb jellies. The weeks either side of the changeover can give you both.

**2. Check the moon.** A bright moon washes out a faint blue flash, whether it comes from plankton or from a jelly. Both seasons are best around the new moon. [The moon calendar](/moon-phase-calendar/) shows the windows month by month, and [the best time guide](/guides/best-time-to-see-bioluminescence/) walks through the full season, moon and weather order.

**3. Watch the water.** Uniform sparkle with every movement means dinoflagellates. Separate shapes flashing one by one means comb jellies.

## Tips for winter nights

- **Give your eyes time.** Full dark adaptation takes about twenty minutes. Individual jellies are much easier to pick out once your eyes have adjusted.
- **Dress for the cold.** Air and water are cooler. The [packing guide](/guides/what-to-wear-and-bring/) covers what to wear by season.
- **Expect calmer water.** Winter nights are often quieter and emptier than summer ones.

## Is the glow safe to be around?

Comb jellies do not sting, so there is nothing to fear from the animals themselves. For the summer show, UF/IFAS Extension says being on a boat or kayak in water with a *Pyrodinium* bloom poses no direct risk to people.

The concern is food, not contact. This species can produce saxitoxin, and its blooms have caused shellfish harvest closures in the lagoon. Pufferfish harvesting is also prohibited there. Do not harvest or eat shellfish from the lagoon during a bloom, and follow current Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission notices.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are comb jellies the same as jellyfish?**
No. Comb jellies are in the phylum Ctenophora and have no stinging cells. Jellyfish are in the phylum Cnidaria.

**Can you see bioluminescence in Florida in winter?**
Yes. From about October to April, the Indian River Lagoon glow comes from comb jellies. It is softer than the summer dinoflagellate glow and shows as separate flashing shapes.

**Do comb jellies glow rainbow colours?**
No. The rainbow is daylight scattered by the beating comb rows. The actual bioluminescence is blue-green and appears only in the dark.

**Which Florida season is brighter?**
Summer. Dinoflagellates light the whole water at once, while winter comb jellies glow one animal at a time.

**When is the best time to see either show?**
Pick the month for the show you want, then book around the new moon, when the sky is darkest.

## Which show should you book?

Neither is better. The summer show is brighter and more dramatic, and it appears in most of the photographs. The winter show is quieter and stranger: glowing shapes you can follow one by one, on calm, cool water.

The mistake is expecting one and getting the other. Choose your month for the show you want, book around the new moon, and judge the night by what is actually in the water.
