## The short answer: pick a dark moon, then check moonset

The best nights to see bioluminescence are the few days either side of the new moon. But the phase alone can mislead you. What matters is how much moonlight is on the water while your tour is out, roughly 8 pm to 11 pm. A fat moon that sets before launch can beat a half moon that hangs overhead all evening.

This guide explains why the moon hides the glow, why moonset times matter as much as the phase, and how to read a moon calendar the way a guide does.

## Why moonlight hides the glow

A bioluminescent flash is weak light. Each dinoflagellate fires for about a tenth of a second, in blue at around 480 nanometres. A paddle stroke only looks bright because thousands of cells fire at once against black water. Turn the black water grey and the same stroke fades into the background. The science of the flash itself is covered in [What is bioluminescence?](/guides/what-is-bioluminescence/)

Moonlight does the greying. It lifts the brightness of the water surface, the sky and the shoreline. Your eyes adapt to whatever light is around them, and full dark adaptation takes at least twenty minutes. A lit sky holds that adjustment partway up, so faint flashes that would stand out on a dark night drop below what you can pick out.

The glow does not get weaker under a moon. Your view of it does.

## Phase is only half the question

Most advice stops at the phase: avoid the full moon, book near the new moon. That is a decent rule and an incomplete one. Phase only tells you how much of the moon is lit. It says nothing about whether the moon is in the sky during your tour.

Look at the two ends of the cycle first:

- **New moon:** it travels with the sun, rising around sunrise and setting around sunset. By the time a tour launches, it is already gone.
- **Full moon:** it does the opposite. It rises around sunset, stays up all night and sets around sunrise, fully lit for every minute of your tour.

At the extremes, the simple rule works. The middle of the cycle is where it breaks down. A half-lit moon sounds like half the problem, but if it sits high in the sky from launch to landing, it lights the whole tour. A fatter moon that sets an hour after sunset can leave most of the same tour in full dark.

What counts is the lit fraction of the moon multiplied by the time it spends above the horizon while you paddle.

## The moonset trick

The moon rises and sets about 50 minutes later each day, on average. That steady drift is what guides actually plan around, and it cuts both ways:

- **A few nights after new moon:** the young crescent sets during the evening. Take the later departure and the moon is gone before you launch.
- **A few nights after full moon:** the waning moon rises late in the evening. Take the early departure and you can be back at the dock before it climbs. The last quarter moon, about a week after full, rises around midnight, so it stays out of the way for the whole evening.
- **The trap is the first quarter moon:** the neat half disc about a week after new moon. It rises around midday and sets around midnight, so it is up for the entire tour window. Half lit, fully present.

So before you write off a date because the calendar shows a big moon, check the set time. Before you book a date because the moon looks thin, check whether it is above the horizon during your tour. For a single night, moonrise and moonset times at your bay tell you more than the phase icon does.

## The best window around the new moon

None of this moves the centre of the target. The few days either side of new moon are still the most reliable nights of the month. The moon is a thin sliver or absent, and what little there is keeps daytime hours.

## How this site scores the moon

The [glow forecast](/glow-forecast/) is built on the same logic. Moon darkness is worth up to 30 of the 100 points in a night's score, but the moon only costs points while it is actually up. The calculation samples the evening at several points across tour hours and checks the moon's lit fraction and whether it is above the horizon at each one.

A bright moon that sets before the tours go out scores far better than its phase alone would suggest. A half moon parked overhead all evening scores worse. The full working, including the season, cloud and wind parts of the score, is set out on the [glow forecast method](/glow-forecast-method/) page.

The short version: the forecast never asks what phase it is. It asks how much moonlight lands on the water while you are paddling.

## How to read the moon calendar, step by step

The [moon phase calendar](/moon-phase-calendar/) lays out each month with its new moon, full moon and dark windows marked. Read it in this order:

1. **Find the new moon** and block out the few days either side. Those are your first-choice dates.
2. **Check the shoulder nights** just outside the window. If the moon is waxing, check the moonset. If it is waning, check the moonrise. Match the dark hours to the departure times on offer.
3. **Cross off the full moon** and the nights right around it. Near full moon, the set time sits close to sunrise, so there is no early escape.
4. **Check the season** for your bay before you settle on a date. A perfect moon in the wrong month is still the wrong month.
5. **Let the forecast settle it** in the final week. Cloud and wind move faster than the moon does.

Season first, then the moon, then the weather. The moon is the middle step, and on most trips it decides whether you get a faint shimmer or water that lights up at every stroke.

## Keep your own night vision

You can lose the dark even on a perfect night. White light ruins night vision for 20 to 30 minutes, so keep phone screens dim and use a red-light headlamp if you need one. Arrive early, give your eyes time to adjust, and the glow will look brighter than it did in the first few minutes.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can you see bioluminescence under a full moon?**
Sometimes, but faintly. Full moonlight lights the whole scene and washes out most of the flash. A new moon gives the strongest contrast.

**Is the new moon always the best night?**
It is the most reliable, but not the only good one. A thin crescent that sets before your tour can be just as dark. Check moonset, not only the phase.

**Which moon phase is worst?**
The full moon is the brightest, but the first quarter is the trap. It is up from about midday to midnight, so it lights the whole tour window.

**How many nights around the new moon are good?**
Plan on about five to six nights centred on the new moon, then check moonset on the edges.

**Does the moon affect the organisms themselves?**
No. The glow does not change. The moon changes how well you can see it.

## Choose your night

Start with the new moon and a window of a few days around it. Then check moonrise and moonset against your departure time, because that decides whether the moon is on the water during the tour. Confirm the season for your bay, and let the forecast and the weather make the final call.
