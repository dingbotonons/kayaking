## The short answer

It depends entirely on the bay. Of all the glowing places covered on this site, exactly one builds its tours around swimming: La Parguera in Puerto Rico. At Mosquito Bay, the brightest of them, swimming is prohibited. At Laguna Grande and on Florida's kayak tours, you stay in the boat. San Diego is a different case again, because its glow is a red tide on an open coast and the question becomes a health advisory, not a tour rule.

The split surprises people. The strict bays are not strict because the water will hurt you. They are strict because the glow itself is fragile, and the rules exist to protect the concentration of organisms that makes it possible.

## Can you swim? Rules by location

| Location | Swimming | What you do instead |
|---|---|---|
| [Mosquito Bay](/puerto-rico/mosquito-bay-vieques/), Vieques | Not allowed | Kayak, trail a hand over the side |
| [Laguna Grande](/puerto-rico/laguna-grande-fajardo/), Fajardo | Not allowed | Kayak through the mangrove channel |
| [La Parguera](/puerto-rico/la-parguera/), Lajas | Generally allowed on most nights, on tours that include it | Boat tour with a swim stop |
| Florida lagoon, [Cocoa Beach](/florida/cocoa-beach/) | Not part of tours | Kayak |
| [San Diego](/california/san-diego/) | Open coast, health advisories apply | Watch from shore |
| Washington, Tomales Bay | No swim tours | Kayak |

## Mosquito Bay: no swimming, by rule

No. [Mosquito Bay](/puerto-rico/mosquito-bay-vieques/) on Vieques is a kayak-only visit. The bay and its mangroves form the Vieques Bioluminescent Bay Natural Reserve, and swimming is not allowed. Tours run with licensed operators.

What you can do is trail a hand over the side of the kayak. Your fingers light up, and a slow drag leaves a bright wake behind them. For most visitors that turns out to be enough contact.

The reason is what washes off you. Natural body oils and chemicals on the skin, including ingredients in sunscreen and insect repellent, can harm the dinoflagellates. Mosquito Bay's glow rests on an unusually dense population packed into a small, enclosed space. One swimmer would not end it. A steady stream of visitors, each trailing chemicals and stirring up the bottom, is a different calculation. The ban keeps the maths on the bay's side.

## Laguna Grande: the tour keeps you in the kayak

[Laguna Grande](/puerto-rico/laguna-grande-fajardo/) at Fajardo works the same way. Discover Puerto Rico states that swimming is not permitted there, to protect the fragile dinoflagellates. You paddle out through the mangrove channel, watch the water light up around the boats, and paddle back.

So the two brightest Puerto Rico bays are both look-but-don't-swim. If getting into the water is the point of your trip, Puerto Rico still has an answer, at the other end of the island.

## La Parguera: yes, and it is the whole trip

[La Parguera](/puerto-rico/la-parguera/), in Lajas on the southwest coast, is the exception. Discover Puerto Rico describes it as the only one of the island's three bio bays where swimming is generally permitted, on most nights. The tours are built around it. The boat runs out after dark, stops in the glowing part of the bay, and you get in.

Three practical points:

- **Book a tour that includes the swim.** Not every La Parguera product is a swim tour. At least one listed water-bike tour says swimming is not allowed. Confirm that yours has a swim stop.
- **Follow the operator's safety rules.** Operators set their own conditions, and the crew decides on the night whether conditions suit it. Ask whether life vests are provided.
- **Keep products off your skin.** The same skin-products rule applies in the water, so skip sunscreen and chemical repellent. Our [what to wear and bring](/guides/what-to-wear-and-bring/) guide covers it.

One honest trade-off: La Parguera does not match Mosquito Bay for brightness. You give up the brightest water in exchange for the only water you can actually enter. The drive is also long, roughly two to three hours from San Juan each way.

## Florida: stay in the boat

The glow is a kayak show. The tours around [Cocoa Beach](/florida/cocoa-beach/) and the Indian River Lagoon paddle out at night and watch from the boat, and a swim stop is not part of the product.

There is a safety reason on top of the product reason. Alligators can be present in Florida's fresh and brackish water, and Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission advice is to swim only in designated swimming areas during daylight hours. An unmarked lagoon at night is not that.

In winter the glowing animals change. From October to April the light comes from comb jellies, drifting animals with no stinging cells at all. A comb jelly cannot sting you. The tours still keep you in the kayak, because the product is a paddle, not a swim. See [Dinoflagellates vs comb jellies](/guides/dinoflagellates-vs-comb-jellies/) for the full split.

## San Diego: a red tide, so check advisories

There is no bay and no swim tour in San Diego. The glow arrives as a [red tide](/california/san-diego/), a bloom of the dinoflagellate *Lingulodinium polyedra* on the open coast, there some years and absent others, with waves lighting up as they break.

Because it is a red tide, the swimming question is a health question. Scripps Institution of Oceanography says most California red tides are not considered dangerous to people, but they can cause skin or eye irritation and respiratory discomfort, especially for people with asthma. Reports also mention minor allergic responses in some people. California public health officials have advised avoiding contact with the bloom. Check California Department of Public Health alerts and local beach advisories before going in, then decide. Treat it like any other advisory day at the beach.

## Washington and Tomales Bay

No swim products exist in either place. The tours are kayak tours, the season runs from summer into autumn, and the water is cold enough that staying in the boat is its own argument. The glow there is something you paddle through, trailing a hand if you want it on your skin.

## Why the strict bays are strict

A glowing bay is a numbers trick. The organisms behind the light, covered in [What is bioluminescence?](/guides/what-is-bioluminescence/), are present in coastal water at some level almost everywhere. A bay glows brightly only when they build up to a density the open sea never reaches. That happens in small, enclosed bays with a narrow mouth, where the water and everything in it stays put.

The rules protect the density. Keep swimmers and their sunscreen out, keep motorboats out, keep visits to licensed operators, and the concentration survives the tourist season. Let everything in, and the bay is still there, still pretty, just dark.

## Is bioluminescent water safe to swim in?

The glow itself is harmless. The dinoflagellates and comb jellies that make it do not bite or sting. The risks come from the place, not the plankton: alligators in Florida, cold water in Washington, and irritation or advisories during a red tide.

One organism deserves a straight mention. *Pyrodinium bahamense*, which lights Puerto Rico's bays and Florida's lagoon, can produce saxitoxin, a neurotoxin, in some populations. Florida's lagoon blooms have caused shellfish harvest closures. Puerto Rico's populations are generally assumed non-toxic, though they are not routinely tested. None of that makes a kayak tour risky. It is one more reason not to swallow the water if you do swim.

## What swimming is like where you can

At La Parguera the crew cuts the engine and the water around the boat starts to sparkle where the hull moves it. Then you slide in.

The difference from a kayak is total. From a boat, the glow is something in front of your eyes. In the water, it is on you. Every kick trails a comet tail of light. A slow breaststroke paints two bright arcs off your hands, and when you float on your back and wave your arms, the water around you lights up as if you were drawing with it. Fish passing below draw their own lines.

It lasts as long as you stay in and keep moving. Then you climb out, and the bay goes back to being something you look at.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can you swim in Mosquito Bay?**
No. Swimming is not allowed in the Vieques Bioluminescent Bay Natural Reserve.

**Can you swim in Laguna Grande?**
No. Tours there are kayak-only, to protect the dinoflagellates.

**Where can you swim in bioluminescent water in Puerto Rico?**
La Parguera, on most nights, on tours that include a swim stop.

**Can you swim in bioluminescence in Florida?**
Tours do not include swimming. FWC advises swimming only in designated areas in daylight, and alligators can be present in brackish water.

**Is it safe to swim in a bioluminescent red tide?**
Most San Diego red tides are not considered dangerous, but they can irritate skin, eyes and lungs. Check public health advisories first.

**Can I wear sunscreen in a bioluminescent bay?**
Operators usually ask you not to. Chemicals on the skin can harm the organisms that make the glow.
